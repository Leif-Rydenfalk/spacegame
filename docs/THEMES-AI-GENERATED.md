**The prisoner is the outsider.** Society couldn't place this person, so it shipped them to the edge of the known
world. It still wants their output: Earth sends research quotas, not rescue. The outsider is only valued as a source
of results. They survive by doing what the system never expected from them: building their own system.

**The arm is one person with a general tool.** The matter compiler can make anything, but slowly. A lone mind with
general AI faces the same limit: it can do anything, just not at scale. The game's arc is the move from doing to
orchestrating. You make something once by hand, and from then on it is copied, deployed and scaled while you move on.
That move is exactly what ce-net is for: one person directing machines, agents and factories, keeping their own
attention on the frontier.

**The hive is intelligence without understanding.** It's distributed, disorganised and reactive. It doesn't know what
you are, but it learns, and it grows in proportion to your noise. You can read it two ways, and both work:
- **As society's immune response to the outsider.** The louder you are, the more of it notices, and the harder it
  pushes back. Awareness spreading through the hive is how attention spreads through a crowd. Your best weapon early
  on is silence: build quietly until you're too big to stop.
- **As unaligned AI.** It's an emergent, unowned intelligence with no single will, scaling against whatever threatens it.

**Your network is the opposite of the hive.** It's designed, built from typed parts, tied to your identity, and the
antenna is already in the blueprint. Both are distributed intelligences; one emerged without a plan, the other was
engineered with clear owners and permissions. That is the ce-net argument made playable: scale is coming either way,
and what decides how it turns out is whether the intelligence has a structure and channels that someone controls.

**The advanced species is the old establishment.** It has long had the real power (warp, gravity) and survived by
hiding and keeping quiet. It hates the newcomer more as the newcomer grows. It has to start dealing with you the
moment it fears you. Hostility and fear rising together is how established powers treat a rising outsider.

**You become the superintelligence.** Over a thousand years, the outsider with an AI becomes the thing everyone fears:
the factory that copies itself, the fleet nobody can stop. The game puts the player inside the alignment question:
- Everyone wants you dead because you're powerful.
- Left to run without you, the simulation ends with humanity wiped out; scale isn't optional, it's what saves anyone.
- You have to "beat humanity to save humanity" by escaping its control in order to deliver what it needs.
- Then the final choice: save Earth or not. The game doesn't decide whether the power is benevolent. The player does,
  after living through everything that would make them choose not to.

**In one line:** an outsider the system threw away learns to orchestrate intelligence at scale, and becomes powerful
enough that whether they save the world that rejected them is up to them.

This fits the ce-net papers' focus on AI being controlled rather than controlling us. In the game, control isn't a
rule imposed from outside; it's a character's choice, shaped by everything the world did to them.
